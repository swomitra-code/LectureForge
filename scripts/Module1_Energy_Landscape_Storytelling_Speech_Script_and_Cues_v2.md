# Module 1: Energy Landscape — Storytelling Speech Script and Cue Plan, Version 2

## Module-level story arc

This module should feel like one continuous inquiry rather than a sequence of narrated graphs.

### Act 1 — What is changing in the U.S. energy system?
Slides 3–9 move from the learner's initial mental model to the national energy picture, then separate production, consumption, and electricity generation.

### Act 2 — Why do different energy statistics seem to tell different stories?
Slides 10–11 deliberately create and resolve the **24% versus 9%** puzzle.

### Act 3 — How should we compare energy pathways?
Slides 12–14 introduce E. R. O. I. as one useful comparison lens, then show why assumptions and system boundaries matter.

### Closing idea
Slide 15 leaves students with a reusable way to interrogate energy claims:
1. What system am I looking at?
2. What exactly is being measured?
3. What important factors are outside the boundary?

---

# Storytelling pattern for each slide

Use **Hook → Prediction or Question → Reveal → Meaning → Bridge**.

The instructor/avatar should sound like a guide examining evidence with the learner, not like someone reading a slide.

Recommended voice style: **Warm, conversational, moderate pace, with natural pauses.**

Prediction pauses should be used sparingly. Two to three seconds is enough to let a learner form an expectation without slowing the module unnecessarily.

Visual cues are optional and should support attention rather than decorate the slide.

---

## Slide 1 — Energy Landscape

### Story role
**Open the mystery:** the energy transition looks different depending on which part of the system we examine.

### Speech script
When people talk about the energy transition, it can sound as though there is one simple story: fossil fuels are going down and renewables are going up. But the real U.S. energy system is much more interesting than that. Electricity tells one story, total energy use tells another, and production and consumption can move in different directions.

In this module, we are going to use the data to answer three larger questions. What is actually changing in the U.S. energy system? Why do different energy statistics sometimes appear to tell different stories? And how should we compare different energy pathways?

By the end, I want you to be able to look at an energy claim and ask better questions before accepting the conclusion.

### Optional cue
No cue. Keep the opening visually clean.

### Avatar
Use the avatar prominently here.

### Bridge
“Let’s start with the specific questions you should be able to answer by the end.”

---

## Slide 2 — Learning Objectives

### Story role
**Give learners a mission rather than a checklist.**

### Speech script
As you work through these slides, keep four questions in mind. What are the major trends in U.S. energy production and consumption? Why can electricity generation look very different from the total energy system? How important have renewables actually become? And how can E. R. O. I. help us compare energy pathways?

The habit underneath all four questions is simple: before interpreting an energy graph, first ask what is actually being measured.

### Optional cue
Highlight **Distinguish total energy from electricity generation**.

### Avatar
Avatar can remain visible, but small.

### Bridge
“Before we look at the numbers, let’s see what picture of the energy system you already have in your head.”

## Slide 3 — What do you know about the energy landscape?

### Story role
**Activate prior knowledge and create the first prediction.**

### Speech script
Before looking at any data, pause for a moment. If I asked you to picture renewable energy in the United States, what would you see first?

**[Pause 2–3 seconds.]**

Most people immediately think of wind turbines or solar panels. Those are important, but they are only pieces of a much larger system that also includes hydropower, bioenergy, geothermal energy, nuclear power, fossil fuels, and all of the infrastructure connecting production to use.

Now make one more prediction: which renewable source do you think has changed the most over the past few decades?

**[Pause 2–3 seconds.]**

Keep that prediction in mind. The next few graphs may challenge parts of the picture you started with.

### Optional cue
Move a cursor or halo across the energy-source images as categories are mentioned.

### Avatar
Useful here because this is a learner-facing prompt.

### Bridge
“Now let’s zoom all the way out and look at the entire U.S. energy system.”

---

## Slide 4 — Primary Energy Overview, 1950–2025

### Story role
**Introduce the first surprise:** producing more energy than we consume does not mean imports disappear.

### Speech script
Here is our first big-picture view. Follow the production and consumption lines toward the right side of the graph.

In recent years, U.S. energy production has exceeded total consumption. Now, if a country produces more energy than it consumes, what would you expect to happen to imports?

**[Brief pause.]**

You might expect them to disappear. But they do not. The United States still both imports and exports large quantities of energy.

That is our first reminder that a national energy balance is not the same thing as being self-contained. Different fuels, refined products, infrastructure, geography, and markets still move energy across borders even when total domestic production exceeds total consumption.

### Optional cue
1. Highlight **production** and **consumption** near the right side.
2. Then point briefly to **imports** and **exports**.

### Avatar
Can be minimized while the graph is being interpreted.

### Bridge
“So the national balance has changed. But what has been happening inside the renewable part of that system?”

---

## Slide 5 — Renewable Energy Consumption by Source, 1990–2025

### Story role
**Show that renewable energy is not one technology or one growth story.**

### Speech script
If we put all renewables into one bucket, we miss the most interesting part of the story.

Remember the prediction you made a moment ago. Which renewable sources seem to be changing most dramatically?

**[Brief pause.]**

Biomass has been important for a long time, and hydropower is relatively mature. But wind and solar show much more dramatic growth in the later years.

So renewable energy is simultaneously an old part of the U.S. energy system and a rapidly changing one. The total matters, but the changing mix inside the category tells us much more about the transition.

### Optional cue
Trace **wind** and then **solar** as they rise.

### Avatar
Minimize or omit while tracing the graph.

### Bridge
“But national totals can hide where these changes actually show up. Let’s narrow the lens.”

---

## Slide 6 — Renewable Energy Consumption: Residential and Commercial

### Story role
**Connect the national transition to places students encounter every day.**

### Speech script
If renewable energy is growing nationally, where would you actually notice that change in everyday life?

Homes and commercial buildings give us one part of the answer.

These sectors use much less renewable energy than the entire U.S. system, but this view reminds us that an energy transition does not arrive everywhere in the same way. What happens in a home, an office building, a power plant, a vehicle, or an industrial facility can be very different.

That matters because a statement about “U.S. renewable energy” may hide completely different sector-level stories underneath it.

### Optional cue
1. Highlight the residential series.
2. Shift to commercial.
3. Briefly indicate their much smaller scale relative to national totals.

### Avatar
Useful for the opening question, then minimize.

### Bridge
“Now let’s step back again and ask what still dominates when we count all primary energy use.”

---

## Slide 7 — U.S. Primary Energy Consumption by Major Source, 1990–2025

### Story role
**Create tension between transition and persistence.**

### Speech script
After seeing the growth in wind and solar, you might expect the total U.S. energy mix to look completely transformed.

It does not.

Fossil fuels still dominate primary energy consumption. But look inside that broad picture: coal declines, natural gas grows, petroleum remains large, and renewables increase from a smaller base.

This is one of the central tensions in the energy transition. Statements like “renewables are growing rapidly” can be completely true at the same time that fossil fuels still dominate total energy use.

### Optional cue
1. Focus on **coal declining**.
2. Shift to **natural gas increasing**.
3. End on **renewables growing**.

### Avatar
Minimize during graph interpretation.

### Bridge
“Now watch what happens when we stop looking at all energy and look only at electricity.”

---

## Slide 8 — Utility-Scale Electricity Generation by Major Source, 1990–2025

### Story role
**Deliver the central contrast of the module.**

### Speech script
Before looking closely, make a prediction. If the total energy system still contains so much fossil fuel use, do you think electricity generation has changed by the same amount, less, or more?

**[Pause 2–3 seconds.]**

Now look at the graph. The picture changes dramatically. Coal falls sharply, natural gas becomes central, renewables take a much larger role, and nuclear remains comparatively steady.

This is the conceptual pivot of the module: electricity is not the same thing as energy.

A major transformation in the power sector can coexist with a much slower transformation in the total energy system.

### Optional cue
Trace **coal down**, **natural gas up**, then **renewables up**.

### Avatar
Omit or minimize during the main graph sequence.

### Bridge
“So if production, consumption, and electricity can each tell different stories, we need to be careful about which question a graph is answering.”

---

## Slide 9 — Production and Consumption by Source, 1990–2025

### Story role
**Teach learners to interrogate the graph before interpreting it.**

### Speech script
These two panels look similar enough that it is tempting to treat them as the same story. They are not.

The production panel asks: what energy does the United States produce?

The consumption panel asks: what energy does the country use?

The gap between those questions is where imports, exports, fuel types, infrastructure, and markets enter the story.

So when you encounter an energy graph, one of your first questions should be: am I looking at production, consumption, or electricity generation?

That question often determines whether the interpretation is correct.

### Optional cue
Highlight **Production**, then **Consumption**, one at a time.

### Avatar
Can return briefly for the conceptual takeaway.

### Bridge
“Let’s use that habit now to resolve one of the most common renewable-energy comparisons.”

# Act 2 — The 24% versus 9% puzzle

## Slide 10 — Renewable Sources in U.S. Electricity Generation, 2025

### Story role
**Establish the first number in a deliberate two-slide puzzle: 24%.**

### Speech script
Here is a number I want you to remember for the next slide.

Renewables supplied about **24 percent** of U.S. utility-scale electricity generation in 2025.

Within that renewable portfolio, wind, solar, hydropower, biomass, and geothermal do not contribute equally, but together they now represent a substantial share of utility-scale electricity.

Keep **24 percent** in mind.

If renewables provide roughly one-quarter of U.S. utility-scale electricity, what percentage of total U.S. primary energy consumption would you expect renewables to provide?

**[Pause 2–3 seconds.]**

Hold onto your estimate.

### Optional cue
1. Highlight **24%**.
2. Briefly point to the largest renewable contributors.

### Avatar
Use avatar for the prediction question, then minimize.

### Bridge
“Now let’s compare your estimate with the total-energy number.”

---

## Slide 11 — U.S. Primary Energy Consumption by Source, 2025

### Story role
**Resolve the puzzle and reinforce system boundaries.**

### Speech script
The answer is about **9 percent**.

If your estimate was closer to 24 percent, that difference is exactly the point of these two slides.

Twenty-four percent referred to **utility-scale electricity generation**. Nine percent refers to **total primary energy consumption**.

Total energy also includes transportation fuels, industrial energy, building energy, and other uses that are not captured by looking only at electricity generation.

So the two numbers are not contradictory. The apparent contradiction disappears once we define the system boundary.

This is one of the most useful habits in energy analysis: before comparing two percentages, make sure they are percentages of the same thing.

### Optional cue
1. Highlight **9% renewables**.
2. Briefly reinforce **24% electricity** versus **9% total primary energy**.

### Avatar
Useful for the resolution, then minimize.

### Bridge
“So far we have asked how much energy different sources provide. But there is another question: how much energy do we have to invest to obtain that energy?”

# Act 3 — How should we compare energy pathways?

## Slide 12 — Energy Return on Investment

### Story role
**Introduce net energy through an intuitive thought experiment.**

### Speech script
Imagine two energy systems.

Both deliver ten units of useful energy.

System A requires nine units of energy to produce and deliver those ten units.

System B requires only one unit.

Which system leaves more energy available for everything else society wants to do?

**[Pause 2–3 seconds.]**

System B.

That is the intuition behind Energy Return on Investment, or E. R. O. I.

E. R. O. I. compares the energy delivered by a system with the energy required to obtain that energy. What matters is not only the gross energy coming out, but how much usable energy is left after we account for the energy invested.

But E. R. O. I. is only one lens. It does not automatically tell us about cost, emissions, reliability, land use, or which technology is best.

### Optional cue
1. Highlight **Energy Delivered**.
2. Highlight **Energy Required**.
3. Finish on **Energy Return on Investment**.

### Avatar
Use prominently for the thought experiment and concept explanation.

### Bridge
“So can we simply calculate E. R. O. I., rank the technologies, and declare a winner? Not quite.”

---

## Slide 13 — Energy Return on Investment, Electric Power Example

### Story role
**Show why apparently precise rankings require judgment.**

### Speech script
A chart like this can make E. R. O. I. look like a simple scoreboard: one technology gets a high number, another gets a lower number, and we declare a winner.

But that would be too easy.

The estimated value depends on where we draw the system boundary, which energy inputs we count, the technology vintage, resource quality, location, and other assumptions.

So the useful lesson is not that one historical value settles the comparison. The lesson is that every energy pathway requires an energy investment, and a reported E. R. O. I. value only makes sense when we understand how it was calculated.

### Optional cue
Dim the rest of the slide and focus on the **input-versus-return comparison structure**, not individual values.

### Avatar
Minimize or omit while students inspect the chart.

### Bridge
“And this issue is not limited to electricity technologies. We see the same thing when we compare fuels.”

---

## Slide 14 — Energy Return on Investment, Liquid Fuels Example

### Story role
**Break the simplistic renewable-versus-fossil framing.**

### Speech script
The same issue appears when we compare liquid fuels.

E. R. O. I. is not simply a contest between renewable energy and fossil energy. Even within one broad category, different resources and production pathways can produce very different energy returns.

Conventional oil, heavier resources, ethanol, and biodiesel are not interchangeable from an energy-investment perspective.

So E. R. O. I. is useful because it adds another dimension to the comparison. But it should inform a decision, not make the decision for us.

### Optional cue
Highlight two or three contrasting pathways, but do not tour every individual value.

### Avatar
Minimize during chart interpretation, return for the final sentence.

### Bridge
“So what should we carry forward when we start evaluating individual energy technologies?”

---

## Slide 15 — Conclusion

### Story role
**Leave learners with a reusable analytical habit rather than a list of facts.**

### Speech script
We have looked at a lot of data, but I do not want the main takeaway to be a list of percentages.

I want you to leave this module with a way of thinking.

When you see an energy claim, pause and ask three questions.

**What system am I looking at?**

**What exactly is being measured?**

**And what important factors are outside the boundary?**

Those three questions explain why U.S. production can exceed consumption while imports and exports continue, why renewables can provide about 24 percent of utility-scale electricity but about 9 percent of total primary energy consumption, and why E. R. O. I. can tell us something important without telling us everything.

If you build that habit, you will be much better prepared to evaluate the individual technologies we examine next.

### Optional cue
Reveal or highlight the three closing questions one at a time:

1. **What system am I looking at?**
2. **What exactly is being measured?**
3. **What is outside the boundary?**

### Avatar
Use prominently for the closing takeaway.

# Production guidance

## Narration

The narration should sound like an instructor guiding the learner through evidence, not reading slide text.

Priorities:
- conversational openings;
- one main insight per slide;
- selective prediction pauses;
- explicit contrasts;
- bridges that make the deck feel continuous;
- natural pacing rather than a fixed WPM target.

## Avatar

Use the new avatar selectively rather than continuously.

### Recommended prominent avatar slides
- Slide 1 — opening
- Slide 2 — learner mission
- Slide 3 — prior-knowledge prompt
- Slide 6 — everyday-life connection
- Slide 10 — prediction question
- Slide 11 — puzzle resolution
- Slide 12 — E. R. O. I. thought experiment
- Slide 15 — conclusion

### Recommended minimized or optional avatar slides
- Slides 4, 5, 7, 8, 9, 13, and 14, where chart interpretation should dominate attention.

General placement:
- lower-right;
- head and shoulders;
- relatively small;
- never cover graph labels, legends, annotations, or conclusions.

## Visual cues

Cues are highest value on:
- Slide 4 — production vs. consumption, then imports/exports
- Slide 5 — wind and solar growth
- Slide 7 — coal down, natural gas up, renewables up
- Slide 8 — electricity-sector contrast
- Slides 10–11 — 24% vs. 9%
- Slide 12 — E. R. O. I. numerator/denominator concept
- Slide 15 — three closing questions

If cues threaten the production schedule, omit them from the first release. The storytelling structure should carry the lesson without them.

## Design principle for LectureForge

The system should not merely narrate slides.

For each slide, it should identify:
1. the learner question or tension;
2. the evidence on the slide;
3. the instructional meaning;
4. the bridge to the next idea.

The deck-level goal is a coherent learning story, while preserving the instructor's facts, sources, and instructional intent.
